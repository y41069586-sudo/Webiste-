# Demo-Seite nach dem QR-Scan: Was Handwerker zum Online-Kauf bringt (Stand 15.11.2026)

Context for the report writer: Offer = Basis (597 € one-time + 19 €/month) or Komplett (0 € setup + 179 €/month for 12 months; per project task list the first Komplett payment is due 7 days after go-live). First-year totals by simple arithmetic: Basis 597 + 12 x 19 = 825 €; Komplett 12 x 179 = 2,148 €. Traffic arrives on mobile from a QR code in a printed letter, and the visitor lands on a pre-built demo of "their" site. Research budget was limited (about 20 tool calls), so several items are marked as gaps instead of being filled from memory.

## Q1: Which on-page elements measurably increase conversion for high-consideration small-business services, and what are the benchmarks?

### Takeaway
Hard, independent evidence exists for only a few levers: page speed (Google/Deloitte), plain and short copy (Unbounce 2024), transparent prices and total cost (NN/g, Baymard), and visible real-company trust signals (Stanford credibility research, Baymard). Most other "tactics" (multi-step forms, exit-intent, guarantees) rest on vendor data with no independent replication. Typical landing-page medians are about 6% for professional services, but these are mostly lead forms, not 600 € purchases, and no reliable scan-to-purchase benchmark exists.

### Cited Findings
**Benchmarks**
- Unbounce 2024 Conversion Benchmark Report (vendor data, 41K+ landing pages, 464M+ visitors, 57M+ conversions): all-industry median landing-page conversion rate 6.6%; industry medians range from 3.8% to 12.3% — [Unbounce Conversion Benchmark Report 2024](https://unbounce.com/conversion-benchmark-report/)
- Unbounce 2024: median for commercial and professional services landing pages is 6.1%. The same page reports desktop converting at 11.6% vs mobile at 8.3% for this segment (figures as reported by search summary; I did not open this sub-page myself) — [Unbounce Professional Services](https://unbounce.com/conversion-benchmark-report/professional-services-conversion-rate/)
- Unbounce 2024: 83% of analysed visits were on mobile; across all industries desktop converts 8% better than mobile — [Unbounce 2024](https://unbounce.com/conversion-benchmark-report/)
- Older Unbounce baseline (2020 report): median 3.2% across all landing pages; the 2020 and 2024 figures are not directly comparable — [Unbounce 2020](https://unbounce.com/conversion-benchmark-report-2020/)
- Direct-mail QR benchmarks are vendor-reported and inconsistent: Stannp reports QR scan rates of about 0.4% to 3.6%, median about 1.63%, top campaigns 5.29%+. One G2-hosted vendor playbook claims a 38% conversion of direct-mail-driven web visits to purchase, with no traceable method (treat as unreliable). Bitly's 7.5% "1,000 scans to 75 purchases" is a worked example, not a benchmark — [Stannp](https://go.stannp.com/blogs/understanding-direct-mail-engagement-rates-what-to-expect-how-to-improve); [G2-hosted playbook PDF](https://images.g2crowd.com/uploads/attachment/file/1744593/The-Tangible-Touchpoint-Playbook---US.pdf); [Bitly](https://bitly.com/blog/track-direct-mail-marketing-results/)

**Copy and readability**
- Unbounce 2024: difficult words (3+ syllables) correlate -24.3% with conversion rate; reading time -19.4%; word count -18.6%. Pages written at a 5th-7th grade reading level convert at 11.1%, 56% higher than 8th-9th grade pages and more than 2x professional-level writing (5.3%) — [Unbounce 2024](https://unbounce.com/conversion-benchmark-report/)

**Price transparency and total cost**
- NN/g (Loranger, 01.12.2013, older qualitative data from "numerous usability studies with business customers"): business customers rank pricing as their top information need; when a site hides prices, participants leave for competitors; "People view companies that hide costs as being evasive and untrustworthy". Recommendation: show prices for typical scenarios or ranges rather than complex configurators — [NN/g: Show Price](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/show-price/); [NN/g: Show prices for common scenarios](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/show-prices-for-common-scenarios/)
- Baymard (page updated 28.10.2026; average of 50 studies published 2006-2025; US e-commerce shoppers): average cart abandonment 70.22%. Reasons: extra costs too high 40%; didn't trust the site with card information 19%; site wanted an account 18%; checkout too long or complicated 17%; returns policy not satisfactory 13%; couldn't see or calculate total cost up front 12%. 42% were "just browsing / not ready to buy" (excluded from the reasons) — [Baymard cart abandonment](https://baymard.com/blog/cart-abandonment-rate)
- Baymard estimates better checkout design alone could raise conversion by 35.26% for the average large e-commerce site (B2C retail context, not services) — [Baymard](https://baymard.com/blog/cart-abandonment-rate)

**Trust signals (real company, real person)**
- Stanford Web Credibility Guidelines: ten guidelines from three years of research with over 4,500 people; one of them is to show that a real organization stands behind the site — [Stanford Web Credibility](https://credibility.stanford.edu/guidelines)
- Secondary summaries of the Stanford research name physical address, phone number and email address as the top credibility signals, followed by photos of the organization's members (secondary, not verified on Stanford's page) — [Userlike summary](https://userlike.com/blog/10-crucial-elements-website-credibility); [University of Washington lecture slides](https://courses.cs.washington.edu/courses/cse100/01sp/lectures/Lec7.pdf); [CXL credibility checklist](https://cxl.com/website-credibility-killers/)

**Exit-intent and pop-ups (vendor data only)**
- Wisepops reports an average exit-intent pop-up CVR of 2.81% (top performers 19.26%); BDOW! (formerly Sumo) reports 3.09% average for all pop-ups and a 2.1% average email opt-in; Popupsmart 3.49% across 10,000+ campaigns. All are vendor-reported, with differing definitions — [Wisepops exit popups](https://wisepops.com/use-cases/exit-popups); [BDOW!](https://bdow.com/?p=4130); [Popupsmart](https://popupsmart.com/blog/do-exit-intent-popups-work)

**Multi-step forms (vendor data only)**
- Formstack-attributed figures (13.9% multi-page vs 4.5% single-page), an "86% better" headline and HubSpot's +59.2% single experiment circulate widely, but I found no independent or peer-reviewed head-to-head study. One A/B test by Conversion Fanatics found similar results for both formats — [HubSpot multi-step forms](https://blog.hubspot.com/marketing/multi-step-forms); [Reform comparison](https://reform.app/blog/single-page-vs-multi-step-forms-which-converts-better); [Leadgen Economy (vendor, source of the 86% figure)](https://www.leadgen-economy.com/blog/multi-step-forms-conversion-optimization/)

**Personalisation and ownership effects (lab research, not website data)**
- IKEA effect (Norton, Mochon & Ariely, Journal of Consumer Psychology 2012): participants who built their own box were willing to pay a 63% premium over an identical pre-assembled box; the effect disappears when the task is not completed — [SSRN: The IKEA Effect](https://papers.ssrn.com/abstract=1777100); [Harvard DASH PDF](https://dash.harvard.edu/bitstream/handle/1/12136084/norton%20mochon%20ariely%20third%20round[1].pdf)

### Inferences
Ranked on-page tactics by expected impact. These rankings are my judgment, combining the evidence above with the specific funnel (mobile QR traffic, Handwerker, 825-2,148 € first-year value):
1. **Very fast mobile load of demo and offer page** (see Q4): this has the strongest causal evidence, and a QR visitor on a phone is the highest-risk bounce case.
2. **A short welcome layer on the first visit, in plain words:** "Das ist ein Entwurf für {Firma}. Noch nicht öffentlich. Online bis {Datum}." Add one sentence on what happens if they buy, and one primary button. This draws on the Unbounce readability data (5th-7th grade language converts best) and on the visitor's need to understand what they are looking at. It is not a directly tested claim.
3. **Full price transparency on one screen, with all costs and the first-year total for each option** (Basis 825 €, Komplett 2,148 € in year 1), plus what is included (domain, hosting, changes). This draws on NN/g and on Baymard's findings that "extra costs" and "couldn't see total cost" drive abandonment.
4. **Real-person and real-company trust block** directly next to the price: photo, name, address, phone, Impressum link, "Rechnung mit Rechnungsnummer". This draws on the Stanford credibility signals, and on Baymard's 19% who did not trust the site with payment data.
5. **Risk reversal phrased as a mechanism, not a slogan** (see Q3), for example "Zahlung erst nach Go-Live" (Komplett) and the 14-day guarantee if active.
6. **A low-commitment second path that is always visible** ("Frage stellen", "Rückruf", "Entwurf ändern lassen", wa.me link) (see Q2).
7. **Honest expiry date** (already in the letter) repeated in the banner. This is consistent with the project's UWG rules. I found no independent effect data within budget (gap).
8. **Return-visit reminder** (banner "Willkommen zurück — Ihr Entwurf ist noch bis {Datum} online") and **exit-intent** have weak evidence. Exit-intent on mobile has no mouse-leave signal, so it would rely on back-button or scroll heuristics. Rank low and A/B test.
- Benchmark expectation: the Unbounce 6.1% professional-services median is for lead forms. A direct purchase of a 597 €+ service from cold postal traffic should be expected far below that. A realistic purchase rate per scan cannot be derived from the sources found.
- The demo is personalised ("their" website, with name and trade). The IKEA effect suggests that involving the visitor in completing or adjusting it (a change request, picking a colour or photo) may raise perceived value, but only if the task is easy to complete. This is a lab finding extrapolated, not tested on websites.

### Gaps
- No independent, published benchmark for scan-to-purchase or scan-to-lead conversion on personalised demo pages. Vendor numbers conflict (1.6% median scan rate vs an untraceable 38% visit-to-purchase).
- No case study found from a "pre-built demo website" vendor with disclosed conversion data (search budget ran out before a dedicated search).
- No CXL or HubSpot primary study on services landing pages fetched; the HubSpot multi-step figure is a single vendor experiment.
- No independent evidence on honest urgency (expiry date) for services. Classic scarcity research (e.g. Lynn 1991 meta-analysis) was not verified within budget.
- No evidence found on effects of a "welcome overlay" or explainer on demo pages specifically.

## Q2: Is "buy now online" realistic for ~600 € services to tradespeople, or do most buyers need an intermediate step?

### Takeaway
B2B buyers increasingly prefer to buy without a sales rep (Gartner: 67% in 2026), but this is mid-to-large-company data. Craft businesses are heavy messenger users (62% use WhatsApp/Telegram) and still value personal reachability. The evidence supports keeping "buy online" as the main path, with a visible, very low-friction "talk to a human" path next to it, not instead of it. Micro-commitment (foot-in-the-door) effects are real but small in the academic literature.

### Cited Findings
- Gartner press release (09.03.2026; 646 B2B buyers, surveyed Aug-Sep 2025): 67% of B2B buyers prefer a rep-free experience, up from 61% in the prior survey (632 buyers, Aug-Sep 2024). An older Gartner page states 75%, with unclear survey details. Secondary reports say Gartner found 69% of buyers turn back to reps to validate AI-generated insights (I could not open the Gartner page, 403; figures via search snippets) — [Gartner press release 2026](https://www.gartner.com/en/newsroom/press-releases/2026-03-09-gartner-sales-survey-finds-67-percent-of-b2b-buyers-prefer-a-rep-free-experience); [Gartner B2B buying journey](https://www.gartner.com/en/sales/insights/b2b-buying-journey); [Demand Gen Report](https://demandgenreport.com/industry-news/news-brief/gartner-67-of-b2b-buyers-prefer-a-rep-free-experience/52142)
- Bitkom Research 2025 (telephone survey, n=504 German craft businesses, representative weighting; report published 2026): all businesses use e-mail, smartphone and landline; 62% use messengers such as WhatsApp or Telegram for internal or external communication; SMS 46%; apps 45%; 77% still use letters; 26% still use fax. 94% have their own website; 88% use online directories; 40% use review platforms; 56% are on social media — [Bitkom Studienbericht Handwerk 2025](https://www.bitkom.org/sites/main/files/2026-01/bitkom-studienbericht-handwerk.pdf)
- Bitkom 2025: 89% of craft businesses see digitalisation positively. 62% call digitalisation a central challenge, 75% name the skilled-labour shortage as a central problem, and 76% say their staff need more digital skills — [Bitkom Studienbericht Handwerk 2025](https://www.bitkom.org/sites/main/files/2026-01/bitkom-studienbericht-handwerk.pdf)
- Foot-in-the-door (Burger 1999, Personality and Social Psychology Review 3(4):303-325): earlier meta-analyses found a relatively small FITD effect (r about .09-.17); the effect depends on conditions, for example when the second request appears as a continuation of the first (secondary summary) — [Burger 1999 via SlideShare copy](https://www.slideshare.net/slideshow/burger/2497673); [CBSM abstract](https://cbsm.com/articles/32623-the-foot-in-the-door-compliance-procedure-a-multiple-process-analysis-and-review)
- Google/Deloitte lead-gen data: a 0.1 s speed gain raised progression from Form Step 1 to Form Submission by 21.6%. This shows that multi-step lead journeys are sensitive to friction — [web.dev: Milliseconds make millions](https://web.dev/case-studies/milliseconds-make-millions)
- Baymard: 42% of abandoners were "just browsing / not ready to buy" (US retail). Many first visits are not purchase-ready sessions — [Baymard](https://baymard.com/blog/cart-abandonment-rate)

### Inferences
- The target group (businesses **without** a website) is the 6% minority in the Bitkom sample. It is plausibly less digitally engaged than the average craft business, which argues for a strong human fallback. This is an inference from the 94% figure, not measured.
- Recommended structure: primary CTA "Seite übernehmen" (to the price cards), secondary CTAs of equal visibility but lower visual weight: (a) "Frage per WhatsApp" (wa.me, visitor-initiated, matches 62% messenger use), (b) "Rückruf anfordern" (phone number plus preferred time, minimal fields), (c) "Entwurf ändern lassen" (free-text). Each secondary step captures contact data with consent and creates a legitimate reason for one personal follow-up.
- Because FITD effects are small, the main value of a micro-step is probably not psychological commitment. It is resolving the specific doubt (price, content, "is this real?") that blocks the purchase.
- Track "scan → any contact or change request" as a leading metric next to "scan → purchase". With small batches (~40 letters), purchases alone will be too rare to learn from.

### Gaps
- No German or Handwerk-specific data on willingness to buy services of 500-2,000 € fully online without contact. ZDH/Bitkom do not report this.
- No data found on conversion of visitor-initiated WhatsApp chats vs forms for German SMB services.
- No two-step funnel study specific to B2B services in the 500-2,000 € range. Available multi-step data is vendor-produced.

## Q3: Best practice for risk reversal and pricing presentation (one-time vs monthly, anchoring, first-year total); German trust factors

### Takeaway
Show every cost upfront and per option, including the first-year total, because hidden or unclear total cost is a documented abandonment driver. Monthly framing makes small amounts feel smaller, but the effect reverses for large sums, so 179 €/month should be paired with an honest total. Risk reversal is theoretically well-founded, but I found no verified effect size. In Germany, invoice-based and PayPal payment dominate consumer e-commerce, and a proper invoice and real company identity are expected trust anchors.

### Cited Findings
- Gourville 1998 "Pennies-a-Day" (Journal of Consumer Research 24(4):395-408): reframing a cost as small daily amounts makes people compare it with small recurring expenses, which improves evaluation and compliance. Gourville 2003 (Marketing Letters): the effect reverses for large amounts ("$1 per day" beats "$365 per year", but "$4,200 per year" beats "$11.50 per day") — [RePEc: Gourville 1998](https://ideas.repec.org/a/oup/jconrs/v24y1998i4p395-408.html); [Springer: Gourville 2003](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1023/A:1025467002310)
- Atlas & Bartels 2018 (JCR): periodic pricing also changes how people mentally represent a contract's benefits, not only its costs — [Atlas & Bartels 2018 PDF](https://home.uchicago.edu/~bartels/papers/Atlas-Bartels-2018-JCR.pdf)
- NN/g: hidden prices are read as evasive and untrustworthy; shoppers assume "If I have to ask, I can't afford it" (2013) — [NN/g](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/show-price/)
- Baymard: 40% abandon over unexpected extra costs; 12% because they couldn't see total cost up front; 13% over an unsatisfactory returns policy (the closest analogue to a guarantee) — [Baymard](https://baymard.com/blog/cart-abandonment-rate)
- Money-back guarantees: academic work exists (e.g. Journal of Retailing 87(4):462-478, 2011, on money-back guarantees, risk perception and willingness to pay a price premium). I could only see the abstract metadata, not effect sizes. Practitioner claims such as "+15-25% purchase rate" could not be verified — [EconPapers: J. Retailing 2011](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:eee:jouret:v:87:y:2011:i:4:p:462-478); [Scinapse: Understanding Money-Back Guarantees](https://www.scinapse.io/papers/2031747054)
- German payments: EHI Online-Payment 2026 (2025 data) reports PayPal at 28.7% and Rechnungskauf at 26.1% of German online retail turnover (B2C) — [IT Finanzmagazin, 08.05.2026](https://www.it-finanzmagazin.de/online-payment-rechnungskauf-und-paypal-weiter-dominierend-laut-ehi-studie-online-payment-2026-244098/)
- Bitkom 2025: 89% of craft businesses observe that their own customers have become clearly more price-sensitive, and 85-87% say customers expect constant reachability and individual offers. This describes Handwerker's view of their customers, which also signals what they themselves value — [Bitkom Studienbericht Handwerk 2025](https://www.bitkom.org/sites/main/files/2026-01/bitkom-studienbericht-handwerk.pdf)

### Inferences
- **Price cards:** show both options side by side without pre-selection (as the project rules already require), each with three lines: setup (597 € / 0 €), monthly (19 € / 179 €), and "im ersten Jahr insgesamt" (825 € / 2,148 €), plus term (Komplett: 12 months) and what happens afterwards. Per Gourville 2003, 179 €/month (a large recurring sum) should not be hidden behind daily framing. Showing the yearly total is both honest and, per that research, not necessarily worse for large sums.
- **Anchoring:** placing Komplett (higher total, 0 € entry) next to Basis makes Basis's 597 € look moderate, and Komplett's 0 € entry lowers the threshold. This is classic anchoring logic, untested here and should be A/B tested. Any "Sie sparen"-type comparison must be factually correct (§ 5 UWG; project rule).
- **Risk reversal, ordered by credibility:** (1) structural, e.g. "Sie zahlen erst, wenn Ihre Seite live ist" (true for Komplett per project task #76; Basis must be checked against the actual checkout flow before it is claimed); (2) "Kostenlose Änderung vor dem Kauf" (exists per task #54); (3) the 14-day guarantee with a concrete mechanism (refund, site offline, domain released). Structural guarantees are more believable than slogans. This is an inference; no study was found comparing guarantee types.
- **German trust factors on the offer page:** Impressum link, full address, phone, the named person with photo, "Sie erhalten eine ordentliche Rechnung", Kleinunternehmer note if applicable, privacy link, and the payment methods shown (SEPA, card). Rechnungskauf/PayPal dominance is B2C data. For a B2B subscription via Stripe, SEPA direct debit is the closest familiar option. "Kauf auf Rechnung" itself is not offered in the current setup.

### Gaps
- No verified effect size for money-back guarantees or "pay after go-live" in B2B services.
- No German study on how Handwerker/KMU evaluate one-time vs subscription website offers.
- No study verified within budget on the legal Impressum duty (§ 5 DDG) or on how much Impressum visibility affects trust. This is a legal requirement, but I fetched no source, so this is left to the legal notes.
- No data on how Kleinunternehmer status (no VAT shown) affects B2B trust; VAT-registered buyers cannot reclaim input VAT either way.

## Q4: How fast should the page load on mobile, and what is the impact of slow pages on conversion?

### Takeaway
The best-known evidence (Google-commissioned, older, 2017-2019 data) shows meaningful conversion and funnel gains from even 0.1 s improvements, and sharply rising bounce probability beyond 1-3 seconds on mobile. For a QR visitor on a phone with a mobile connection, the demo should render its first screen in about 1-2 seconds.

### Cited Findings
- "Milliseconds Make Millions" (commissioned by Google, run by 55 and Deloitte Digital; load times monitored for 30 days in late 2019; 37 European and US brand sites; 30M+ sessions): a 0.1 s mobile speed improvement raised conversion rates by 8.4% (retail) and 10.1% (travel). For lead generation it raised Homepage-to-Form-Step-1 progression by 5.5%, Form-Step-1-to-Submission by 21.6% and page views by 7%. Luxury: Contact-Us progression +20.6%. Note that this is a vendor-commissioned study — [web.dev case study](https://web.dev/case-studies/milliseconds-make-millions); [Deloitte report PDF](https://www2.deloitte.com/content/dam/Deloitte/ie/Documents/Consulting/Milliseconds_Make_Millions_report.pdf)
- Google/SOASTA (2017, machine-learning model on mobile benchmarks): the probability of bounce rises 32% as mobile page load time goes from 1 s to 3 s, 90% at 1 s to 5 s, 106% at 1 s to 6 s, and 123% at 1 s to 10 s. These are older data and I found no current update — [Think with Google](https://thinkwithgoogle.com/data/page-load-time-statistics); [Instapage method summary](https://instapage.com/blog/optimizing-mobile-page-speed)
- Unbounce 2024: 83% of landing-page visits are mobile, and mobile converts worse than desktop (desktop +8% across industries) — [Unbounce 2024](https://unbounce.com/conversion-benchmark-report/)

### Inferences
- Target: the first meaningful view of the demo in under about 2 s on a mid-range phone over 4G, measured as LCP in field data, plus a stable layout (no banner jumping in). The 1-to-3-second bounce curve makes the first second the most valuable. The exact threshold is an inference; Google's thresholds for Core Web Vitals were not re-checked within budget.
- Practical implications for this stack: static pre-rendered demo HTML, compressed hero image with fixed dimensions, no third-party chat widget loaded on first paint (a wa.me link costs nothing), and Stripe loaded only on the checkout step.
- QR traffic is all mobile and often arrives in the moment of reading the letter. Every extra redirect hop (short URL → /q/token → demo) adds latency and should stay a single fast 302.

### Gaps
- No post-2020 independent large-scale study on mobile speed vs conversion was found within budget. The figures above are Google-commissioned and from 2017-2019.
- No data specific to German mobile network conditions or to small-business sites.
